Johannes Gutenberg combined movable metal type with a press in Europe during the mid-fifteenth century. Scribes had previously copied books by hand, which made reproduction slow and expensive. Printing enabled many copies to be produced from one prepared set of type and decreased the cost of reproducing a book.

Cheaper, more numerous books reached a broader reading public. Printers also produced pamphlets in vernacular languages, allowing religious and political arguments to circulate more quickly than handwritten copies. The spread of printed material did not create literacy by itself, but it supported growing demand for reading and education.

Authorities responded after printed controversy had expanded. Some governments and churches required licenses or censored printers, while printers sometimes moved production to places with fewer constraints. Printing therefore changed both the speed of communication and the attempts to control it.
